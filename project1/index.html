01

Three exposures, one photograph

Prokudin-Gorskii recorded scenes through blue, green and red filters on separate exposures. The digitised glass plate places those exposures in a single vertical strip. Their positions differ slightly, so simply combining them produces coloured outlines along edges.

The reconstruction uses a translation model: keep blue fixed, then move green and red until their image content agrees with blue. Every supplied scan is read at its original resolution. Integer pixels are converted to floating point by dividing by the maximum value of their storage type, 255 for 8-bit data or 65,535 for 16-bit data.

For a scan of height H, each band has height floor(H/3). The first band is blue, the second green and the third red. Any leftover rows at the bottom are discarded. The aligned output is assembled in the order red, green, blue.

Offset convention. All pairs are (x, y), in pixels, and describe the shift applied to the moving channel. Positive x moves right; positive y moves down. A moving pixel sampled at (y − dy, x − dx) is compared with blue at (y, x).

Scoring ignores the outer 12% of each axis and increases that margin when needed to keep all candidates inside the image. This scoring mask is separate from the optional border-detection step. The final translation uses array rolling, then removes the wrapped regions by keeping only the valid overlap of all three channels.

02

A direct search at one scale

The single-scale version tries every integer displacement in a user-specified square window. With the default radius of 15, there are 31 × 31 = 961 candidates for each moving channel. Two explicit loops enumerate the horizontal and vertical shifts.

Normalised cross-correlation

For each candidate, A is the fixed blue interior and B is the corresponding shifted green or red interior. Subtracting their means removes a constant brightness difference. Dividing by their lengths removes a positive scale difference.

NCC(A, B) = Σ[(A − mean(A))(B − mean(B))] / √{Σ(A − mean(A))² · Σ(B − mean(B))²}

The displacement with the largest NCC is selected. All candidates at a given search level use the same reference region, so changing the amount of overlap does not make a candidate artificially attractive. The code also supports L2 through mean squared error, which has the same optimum as the L2 norm on a fixed-size region. All report results use NCC.

NCC is useful across exposures with different gain and brightness. It does not solve every cross-channel difference: a blue garment can be bright in one channel and dark in another.

03

Coarse-to-fine alignment

A high-resolution channel contains roughly 12 million pixels, and its displacement can exceed 100 pixels. Searching a large window at every full-resolution pixel would be expensive. The pyramid first solves a smaller version of the problem, then refines that answer at successively larger scales.

  1. Low-pass filter and downsample. Apply the separable kernel [1, 4, 6, 4, 1] / 16 along each axis, with reflected boundaries. Keep every second pixel. The filter suppresses high frequencies before subsampling.
  2. Build the levels explicitly. Repeat until the longest dimension is at most 240 pixels. No library pyramid function is used.
  3. Search the coarsest level. Use the same exhaustive raw-pixel NCC search over [−15, 15] in both directions.
  4. Propagate and refine. Double the current displacement at the next finer level, then search a local [−3, 3] window around it. The local correction absorbs rounding errors and adds detail.
  5. Finish at the original scale. Repeat through level zero and apply the resulting integer displacement to the original, full-resolution channel.

At fine levels, the score uses a regular sampling stride, capped at approximately 400 samples along each axis. The images and displacement grid remain at full resolution. This reduces score computation without rounding the final shift to the sampling stride. Every low-resolution interior pixel is used.

06

Refinements and their limits

1. Gradient features

Emir exposes the weakness of matching brightness across colour channels. The blue robe has very different intensity in the red and blue exposures. Raw NCC selects R (-203, 139), which creates a conspicuous duplicate figure.

The improved version computes horizontal and vertical central differences at each pyramid level, then forms gradient magnitude √(gx² + gy²). NCC is applied to those magnitudes. Object boundaries remain useful even when the interior colour changes strongly between channels. The pyramid and search windows are unchanged.

The resulting offsets are G (23, 49) and R (40, 107). The head, robe and chair now agree much more closely. The result still contains small local colour differences because translation alone cannot account for all scene motion or optical differences.

2. Detecting scan borders

Removing invalid translated support does not remove the physical frame recorded in the scan. The crop detector searches the outer 12% for bands dominated by very dark pixels, nearly white pixels or strong disagreement between channels. It forms row and column profiles using the middle 70% of the opposite axis, smooths each profile over three samples, and compares it with a threshold derived from the central scene.

The last detected anomalous band determines each crop boundary. There is no fixed amount removed from each side. The threshold has a floor of 0.30 and otherwise uses the central median plus four median absolute deviations. Pixel evidence is defined by all channels below 0.08, all channels above 0.92, or a channel range above 0.70.

This conservative detector removes large scan rails while preserving more scene content. It can leave thin coloured lines and isolated damaged patches, especially in Self portrait. It does not attempt to repair the photograph itself.

3. Automatic white balance

A gray-world estimate treats the average scene colour as a neutral reference. After cropping, pixels with mean intensity between 0.10 and 0.90 are sampled. For each channel, the gain is the mean of the three channel means divided by that channel’s mean. Gains are restricted to [0.80, 1.25] to limit large colour shifts, then values are clipped to [0, 1].

This reduces the cool cast in Church, but the assumption is unreliable for strongly coloured scenes. In Siren, blue flowers are part of the scene rather than proof of blue illumination. The principal refined gallery therefore shows aligned and cropped images; tonal variants are presented here as separate experiments.

Church gains (R, G, B): (1.1621, 1.1307, 0.8).

4. Automatic contrast

The final tonal variant uses a shared percentile stretch. Let a and b be the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles of all sampled channel values. Apply clip((I − a) / (b − a), 0, 1) to every channel. The shared mapping avoids the independent channel stretches that would introduce additional colour changes.

Percentiles reduce the influence of a few damaged or saturated pixels. The stretch increases tonal separation, although clipping can remove some highlight and shadow detail. It is a display adjustment, not an estimate of the scene’s original colours.

Checks and performance

The run used Python 3.12.14, NumPy 2.3.5 and Pillow 12.3.0 on Windows 11. The median raw-NCC alignment time across the 14 TIFF inputs was 3.70 seconds. The longest complete per-image run, including both methods, optional enhancements and writing full-resolution and webpage JPEGs, was 42.37 seconds. Timings depend on the machine and concurrent work.

Five automated tests passed. They cover signed shifts with both NCC and L2; gain and brightness changes under NCC; large displacements on odd-sized images; RGB order and valid support after rolling; and BGR splitting with 8-bit and 16-bit normalisation. The three JPEGs also give identical single-scale and pyramid offsets.

What remains imperfect

The raw baseline fails on Emir. Gradient features correct its large displacement, but there is no ground-truth registration against which to claim zero pixel error. Leaves, water and people can move between exposures. Local colour traces can also arise from distortion, uneven exposure and damage to the original plate. A single translation cannot correct these effects. The crop detector is deliberately conservative and retains some narrow border remnants.

The most useful improvement is changing the alignment features. Colour adjustments change the appearance of an already aligned image; they cannot repair a geometric error.
