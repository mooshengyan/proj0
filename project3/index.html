Part A

Working with a pretrained flow model

Image restoration, guided editing and two ways to combine velocity fields.

PixNerd predicts a velocity at the current image and time. The clean endpoint has t = 1 and Gaussian noise has t = 0. Each training pair defines a straight path, but the direction predicted during sampling can change after every update.

Training path: xt = (1 − t)ε + tx1,   ε ∼ N(0, I)Target velocity: vtarget = x1 − εSampling equation: dx(t)/dt = vθ(x(t), t, c)

The inputs are center-cropped and resized to 512 × 512, then mapped to RGB values in [−1, 1]. Computation and integration use float32. Images are clipped only for display. The prompt embeddings are cached, and no model training is performed in this part.

Part A · 1.1

Constructing noisy inputs

I use the building photograph throughout the restoration experiments. One Gaussian noise tensor, generated with seed 180, is shared by all three starting times. This keeps the underlying noise realization fixed as the amount of image information changes.

At t = 0.25, the roofline is still visible, but the window details are largely lost in the noise. At t = 0.50, the building and trees become easier to distinguish. At t = 0.75, the photograph is clearly recognizable, although fine grain remains. The noise pattern is fixed across all three images, so this comparison changes only the interpolation weight.

Part A · 1.3

One prediction to the endpoint

For one-step denoising, I predict a velocity once and extrapolate to t = 1. The network predicts a direction of travel, so the update adds the remaining time multiplied by velocity. The condition is the exact prompt a high quality photo.

x̂1 = xt + (1 − t)vθ(xt, t, c)

A single model prediction removes far more visible noise than Gaussian smoothing while retaining the building's main structure. At t = 0.25, it recovers the facade and window layout, but the wall panels become smooth and the tree becomes a soft mass. At t = 0.50, window frames and shutters are clearer. At t = 0.75, more foliage and wall texture survive, although the result still loses some fine detail from the original.

Raw reconstruction MSE decreases from 0.016602 to 0.006421 to 0.002658 as the starting time increases. Each value is lower than the selected Gaussian baseline at the same time. The clean-looking output at t = 0.25 should not be read as exact recovery: the model replaces details that the noisy input no longer specifies reliably.

Part A · 1.4

What the model adds

Because the original and sampled noise are known, I can compare the predicted image update with the update that exactly reaches the original. The heatmap measures the root-mean-square difference across the three RGB channels at each pixel.

Δpred = (1 − t)vθ(xt, t, c)Δtrue = (1 − t)(x1 − ε) = x1 − xtEt = √meanRGB[(Δpred − Δtrue)2]

All update images use one symmetric RGB range, [−3.952, 3.952], with zero at middle gray. All error heatmaps share the same linear range, 0 to 1.491. Errors are computed before display conversion.

The predicted and ground-truth update images look similar because both largely cancel the same noise. The shared-scale error maps reveal where they disagree: roof openings, window frames, shutters and foliage stand out more than the broad sky and wall regions. The strongest errors occur at t = 0.25, where the one-step result also loses the most detail.

Mean per-pixel RGB RMS update error is 0.087769, 0.054740 and 0.036182 at t = 0.25, 0.50 and 0.75. These are errors in the remaining update, not in the unscaled velocity. The factor 1 - t becomes smaller as time increases, so it contributes to the decrease. All maps use the same linear range, allowing their brightness to be compared directly.

Part A · 1.5

Following the velocity field

Euler sampling makes a small update, then evaluates the model again at the new image and time. Starting from the saved t = 0.50 image, 25 updates of h = 0.02 reach t = 1. The implementation shortens the last step when a starting time does not divide evenly into this schedule.

xt+h = xt + hvθ(xt, t, c)

Part A · 1.6

Generating from noise

Starting at t = 0 removes the original image entirely. Each example below begins with independent Gaussian noise and uses 50 Euler updates, h = 0.02, with a high quality photo. These are ordinary conditional samples, without classifier-free guidance.

The five noise seeds produce different portraits under the same broad prompt. Seeds 182 and 184 give the clearest faces. Seed 180 has missing facial structure, seed 183 has distorted features and irregular skin texture, and seed 181 has an awkward transition between clothing and the torso. Ordinary conditional sampling can therefore produce recognizable subjects without producing a convincing photograph every time.

All five samples are portraits even though the prompt only asks for a high quality photo. The results vary in pose, hair, clothing and background, but this small set shows limited variety in subject matter. Each image starts from independent Gaussian noise and uses the same 50-step Euler sampler.

Part A · 2.1

Classifier-free guidance

I evaluate the same state twice: once with the photo prompt and once with the empty prompt. Part A defines guidance around the conditional prediction. With w = 7, the difference from the unconditional field is added seven more times.

vCFG = vc + w(vc − vu),   w = 7

Each row uses the same initial noise for its two results. Step size remains 0.02. Setting w = 0 recovers ordinary conditional sampling.

With guidance weight 7, all five matched results have more coherent facial structure and cleaner backgrounds. The difference is especially clear for seeds 180 and 183, whose unguided conditional samples have substantial facial distortions. Seeds 181 and 182 also gain stronger contrast and more distinct facial details. The guided results still have smooth, polished skin, so sharper features do not remove every sign of synthesis.

Each pair uses exactly the same initial noise. Guidance changes the velocity field throughout sampling, so it can also change appearance, pose and framing. This set demonstrates improved visual coherence, while its subject variety remains limited to portraits.

Part B

Learning flow matching from scratch

A progression from one-step denoising to time-conditioned and class-conditioned MNIST generation.

All optimization uses the full 60,000-image MNIST training split. Test images are held out, and pixels are normalized to [0, 1]. Training batches are shuffled, and fresh Gaussian noise is generated whenever a batch is fetched. All reported losses are averaged over images and pixels. The five experiments start as separate training runs.

Loss plots retain every batch and use a logarithmic MSE axis so both early spikes and later convergence remain visible.

Part B · 1.1

The denoising U-Net

The U-Net follows the supplied architecture. Downsampling moves from 28 × 28 to 14 × 14 and 7 × 7. Average pooling collapses the bottleneck to 1 × 1; transposed convolutions recover spatial resolution. Skip connections concatenate matching encoder and decoder features.

Part B · 1.2.3

What pure-noise denoising learns

A second U-Net is trained with independent Gaussian noise as input and a clean training digit as target. It uses the same five-epoch denoising setup. In this setting the input contains no information about which target digit was selected.

z = ε,   ε independent of xargminD E[‖D(ε) − x‖2] = E[x | ε] = E[x]

The MSE-optimal prediction is therefore the training distribution mean. This explains why a successful optimization need not produce a distinct digit from each noise input.

Part B · 2.1

Adding time conditioning

Flow matching changes the target from a clean image to a velocity. I draw x0 from Gaussian noise, choose a clean training image x1, sample t uniformly on [0, 1] and construct the interpolated state. The target is x1 − x0.

xt = (1 − t)x0 + tx1L = E[‖uθ(xt, t) − (x1 − x0)‖2]

Two fully connected blocks embed the scalar time: Linear, GELU, then Linear. Their output widths are 2D and D. These embeddings multiply the features immediately after unflattening and immediately after the first upsampling block.

h7 ← h7 ⊙ FC1(t)h14 ← h14 ⊙ FC2(t)
Part B · 2.2

Training the time-conditioned model

The time-conditioned model uses D = 64, batch size 64 and Adam with initial learning rate 0.01. I train for 10 epochs. After each epoch, ExponentialLR multiplies the learning rate by 0.11/10. Noise and continuous times are sampled again for every fetched batch.

Part B · 2.3

Part B · 2.4

Adding digit classes

Class conditioning represents the digit as a ten-element one-hot vector. Two additional fully connected blocks produce class embeddings. During training, the entire class vector is set to zero with probability 0.1. The zero vector is an unconditional input, distinct from the one-hot vector for digit zero.

h7 ← FCc1(c) ⊙ h7 + FCt1(t)h14 ← FCc2(c) ⊙ h14 + FCt2(t)
Part B · 2.5

Training with class dropout

The class-conditioned model uses the same flow target and optimizer schedule as the time-only model, with D = 64, batch size 64 and 10 epochs. Class dropout teaches one network to predict both conditional and unconditional fields.

Part B · 2.6

Part B · 2.6 · Scheduler experiment

Training without a scheduler

I train another class-conditioned model from a fresh initialization, using the same initialization seed, data order and ten-epoch budget. To replace the scheduler, I lower Adam's learning rate from the scheduled run's initial 0.01 to a constant 0.001. The rate remains fixed for all 10 epochs. This trades the large initial updates for a step size that can remain stable late in training.

The comparison separates optimization error from visual quality. A lower held-out flow MSE alone does not establish more readable digits or better class accuracy.

Removing the scheduler still produces recognizable digits when the learning rate is reduced to a constant 0.001. Its epoch-1 samples are already more coherent than the scheduled run's first samples. By epoch 10, both runs produce the requested classes with similar overall quality. Small differences remain in stroke thickness, loop shape, and occasional extra marks, such as the short tail beside the first constant-rate 2.

The constant-rate model's held-out velocity MSE is 0.102781, 0.087275, and 0.081485 at epochs 1, 5, and 10. The scheduled model finishes at 0.081030. This small final difference does not establish a visual quality ranking. The comparison changes both the initial learning rate and its schedule: constant 0.001 replaces an initial 0.01 with exponential decay. Both runs share initialization, data order, random training draws, sample noise, labels, guidance, and Euler step count.

Reflection

What I learned

The clearest lesson was that numerical error and visual quality answer different questions. On the building photograph, Euler sampling restores sharper texture but has a higher MSE than the one-step estimate. The pure-noise denoiser makes the same point more strongly: it learns the average training image because that is the best prediction under squared error when the input gives no information about the target.

Conditioning changes what the model can recover from noise. The time-only MNIST model produces digits without choosing their identities, while class conditioning makes the requested 0 through 9 grid possible. Removing the scheduler still gives comparable final samples when the learning rate is reduced to 0.001. For the image illusions, the mathematical construction alone does not guarantee a convincing result. The unsuccessful cat-and-forest trials made it necessary to judge both viewing scales before choosing the final hybrids.
